Unit I

Linear Algebra

1. Vector Space

DEFINITION
A non-empty set \(V\) over a field \(F\), with vector addition and scalar multiplication, is a vector space if it is an abelian group under addition and scalar multiplication satisfies distributivity, associativity and identity: \(a(u+v)=au+av\), \((a+b)v=av+bv\), \((ab)v=a(bv)\), and \(1v=v\).

Meaning: Vectors can be added and multiplied by scalars while remaining inside the set. Examples include \(F^n\), polynomial space \(F[x]\), and spaces of matrices.

Subspace test. A non-empty subset \(W\subseteq V\) is a subspace iff for every \(u,v\in W\) and \(a,b\in F\), \(au+bv\in W\).

Why the subspace test works

1. Since \(W\) is non-empty, choose \(w\in W\). Taking \(a=b=0\) gives \(0\in W\).
2. Taking coefficients \(1,-1\), closure gives \(u-v\in W\); hence additive inverses exist in \(W\).
3. Taking \(b=0\) gives closure under scalar multiplication; taking \(a=b=1\) gives closure under addition.
4. All remaining vector-space axioms are inherited from \(V\). Thus \(W\) is a vector space.

Example: \(W=\{(x,y,0):x,y\in F\}\) is a subspace of \(F^3\), while \(\{(x,y):x+y=1\}\) is not, because it does not contain the zero vector.

2. Linear Homomorphism, Kernel and Range

DEFINITION
A map \(T:U\to V\) is linear if \(T(u_1+u_2)=T(u_1)+T(u_2)\) and \(T(au)=aT(u)\) for all vectors and scalars.

Kernel: \(\ker T=\{u\in U:T(u)=0\}\). Range: \(T(U)=\{T(u):u\in U\}\). Both are subspaces of their respective spaces.

Kernel is a subspace

For \(x,y\in\ker T\), \(T(x)=T(y)=0\). For scalars \(a,b\), \(T(ax+by)=aT(x)+bT(y)=0\). Thus \(ax+by\in\ker T\), proving the claim.

Range is a subspace

Take \(p=T(x)\) and \(q=T(y)\) in the range. Then \(ap+bq=aT(x)+bT(y)=T(ax+by)\), which is again in the range.

3. Quotient Space and First Isomorphism Theorem

DEFINITION + THEOREM
For a subspace \(W\le V\), the coset \(v+W=\{v+w:w\in W\}\). The quotient space \(V/W\) is the set of all cosets, with \((u+W)+(v+W)=(u+v)+W\) and \(a(v+W)=av+W\).
First Isomorphism Theorem. If \(T:U\to V\) is linear, then \(U/\ker T\cong \operatorname{range}T\). If \(T\) is onto, this becomes \(U/\ker T\cong V\).

Proof

Define \(\Phi:U/\ker T\to\operatorname{range}T\) by \(\Phi(u+\ker T)=T(u)\).
1. Well-defined: If \(u+\ker T=v+\ker T\), then \(u-v\in\ker T\). Hence \(T(u)-T(v)=T(u-v)=0\), so \(T(u)=T(v)\).
2. Linear: \(\Phi(a(u+K)+b(v+K))=T(au+bv)=aT(u)+bT(v)\).
3. Injective: If \(\Phi(u+K)=0\), then \(T(u)=0\), so \(u\in K\) and \(u+K=K\).
4. Surjective: Every element of the range has the form \(T(u)=\Phi(u+K)\). Therefore \(\Phi\) is an isomorphism.

4. Internal Direct Sum and External Direct Product

DEFINITION
The sum \(V=U_1+\cdots+U_r\) is direct, written \(V=U_1\oplus\cdots\oplus U_r\), when every vector has a unique decomposition \(v=u_1+\cdots+u_r\), with \(u_i\in U_i\).

The external direct product consists of tuples \((u_1,\ldots,u_r)\), with componentwise addition and scalar multiplication.

Internal direct sum is isomorphic to external direct product

Define \(\Psi(v)=(u_1,\ldots,u_r)\) where \(v=\sum u_i\) is the unique decomposition. Uniqueness makes \(\Psi\) well-defined. Componentwise operations show linearity. If \(\Psi(v)=0\), every \(u_i=0\), so \(v=0\), hence injective. Every tuple determines \(v=\sum u_i\), hence surjective. Thus \(\Psi\) is an isomorphism.

5. Linear Combination, Span, Independence and Basis

DEFINITIONS + THEOREMS
A linear combination of \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\) is \(a_1v_1+\cdots+a_nv_n\). The span \(L(S)\) is the set of all finite linear combinations of elements of \(S\).
The vectors are linearly independent if \(a_1v_1+\cdots+a_nv_n=0\) forces all \(a_i=0\). A basis is a linearly independent spanning set.

Uniqueness of coordinates in a basis

Suppose \(v=\sum a_iv_i=\sum b_iv_i\). Subtract: \(\sum(a_i-b_i)v_i=0\). Independence gives \(a_i-b_i=0\) for each \(i\), so \(a_i=b_i\). Thus coordinates are unique.

Every linearly independent set in a finite-dimensional space can be extended to a basis

Start with independent vectors \(v_1,\ldots,v_k\). If they do not span \(V\), choose \(v_{k+1}\) outside their span. Adding it preserves independence. Repeat. Since \(V\) is finite-dimensional, this process must stop after finitely many steps; at termination the set is independent and spans \(V\), hence is a basis.

All bases of a finite-dimensional vector space have the same number of elements

Let one basis have \(m\) elements and another have \(n\). The replacement/linear-independence lemma says an independent set cannot have more elements than a spanning set. Thus \(m\le n\) and, reversing their roles, \(n\le m\). Hence \(m=n\). This common number is \(\dim V\).
Dimension formula: If \(U,W\) are finite-dimensional subspaces, then \(\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U\cap W)\). In particular, if the sum is direct, dimensions add.

6. Dual Space and Dual Basis

DEFINITION + THEOREM
The dual space is \(V^*=\operatorname{Hom}_F(V,F)\), the vector space of all linear functionals from \(V\) to \(F\). If \(B=(v_1,\ldots,v_n)\) is a basis, its dual basis \((f_1,\ldots,f_n)\) is defined by \(f_i(v_j)=\delta_{ij}\).

Theorem: \(\dim V^*=\dim V=n\)

Let \(v_1,\ldots,v_n\) be a basis. Every \(v\in V\) has a unique expansion \(v=\sum a_jv_j\). Define \(f_i(v)=a_i\). Each \(f_i\) is linear and \(f_i(v_j)=\delta_{ij}\). If \(\sum c_if_i=0\), evaluating at \(v_j\) gives \(c_j=0\); hence the functionals are independent. For any \(f\in V^*\), \(f(v)=\sum a_jf(v_j)=\sum f(v_j)f_j(v)\), so \(f=\sum f(v_j)f_j\). They span; therefore they form a basis of \(V^*\), and its dimension is \(n\).

Example: On \(\mathbb R^2\), for the standard basis, \(f_1(x,y)=x\), \(f_2(x,y)=y\). Hence \(f_2(3,7)=7\).

7. Annihilator

DEFINITION + THEOREM
For \(W\le V\), the annihilator is \(W^0=\{f\in V^*:f(w)=0\text{ for every }w\in W\}\).

Dimension of the annihilator

Let \(\dim V=n\) and \(\dim W=k\). Choose a basis \(w_1,\ldots,w_k\) of \(W\), extend it to a basis \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\) of \(V\), and take the corresponding dual basis \(f_1,\ldots,f_n\). A functional vanishes on all of \(W\) exactly when its first \(k\) dual coordinates are zero. Thus \(W^0=\operatorname{span}\{f_{k+1},\ldots,f_n\}\), so \(\dim W^0=n-k=\dim V-\dim W\).

Double annihilator: In finite dimension, identifying \(V\) with \(V^{**}\), the annihilator of \(W^0\) in \(V^{**}\) corresponds exactly to \(W\).

8. Homogeneous Linear Systems

THEOREM + PROOF
For a homogeneous system \(Ax=0\), with \(A\) an \(m\times n\) matrix of rank \(r\), the solution space has dimension \(n-r\). Therefore it has \(n-r\) linearly independent basis solutions.

Proof using rank-nullity

Regard \(A\) as a linear map \(T:F^n\to F^m\), \(T(x)=Ax\). Its kernel is precisely the solution space, and its range has dimension \(r\), the rank of \(A\). By rank-nullity, \(\dim F^n=\dim\ker T+\dim\operatorname{range}T\). Hence \(n=\dim\ker T+r\), so the solution space dimension is \(n-r\).

9. Space of Linear Transformations

THEOREM + PROOF
If \(\dim V=m\) and \(\dim W=n\), then \(\dim\operatorname{Hom}(V,W)=mn\).

Proof by matrix-unit maps

Choose bases \(v_1,\ldots,v_m\) and \(w_1,\ldots,w_n\). Define \(E_{ij}(v_k)=\delta_{ik}w_j\). For any linear map \(S:V\to W\), write \(S(v_i)=\sum_j a_{ij}w_j\). Then \(S=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}E_{ij}\), so these maps span. If \(\sum c_{ij}E_{ij}=0\), applying to \(v_i\) gives \(\sum_jc_{ij}w_j=0\); independence of the \(w_j\) gives every \(c_{ij}=0\). Thus there are \(mn\) basis maps.

10. Scalar identities, basis extension and dimension results

LEMMAS + COROLLARIES
For every vector space, \(a0=0\), \(0v=0\), \((-a)v=-(av)\); if \(v\ne0\) and \(av=0\), then \(a=0\). These follow directly from the vector-space axioms and additive inverses.

Dimension consequences: If \(W\le V\) and \(V\) is finite-dimensional, then a basis of \(W\) can be extended to a basis of \(V\). Consequently \(\dim(V/W)=\dim V-\dim W\). For subspaces \(U,W\), \(\dim(U+W)=\dim U+\dim W-\dim(U\cap W)\).

11. Dual separation property

LEMMA
If \(V\) is finite-dimensional and \(v\ne0\), there exists \(f\in V^*\) such that \(f(v)\ne0\).

Proof

Extend the one-element independent set \(\{v\}\) to a basis \((v,v_2,\ldots,v_n)\). Define the coordinate functional \(f(a_1v+a_2v_2+\cdots+a_nv_n)=a_1\). It is linear and \(f(v)=1 e0\).

Unit II

Inner Product Spaces

1. Inner Product and Norm

DEFINITION
On a real vector space, an inner product is a map \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to\mathbb R\) that is linear in each variable, symmetric, and positive definite: \(\langle v,v\rangle\ge0\), with equality iff \(v=0\). For complex spaces use conjugate symmetry and the chosen convention of linearity.

The induced norm is \(\|v\|=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}\). Two vectors are orthogonal if \(\langle u,v\rangle=0\).

2. Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality

THEOREM + PROOF
\(|\langle u,v\rangle|\le\|u\|\,\|v\|\).

Proof (real inner-product space)

If \(v=0\), the result is immediate. Otherwise, positivity gives \(0\le\|u-tv\|^2\) for every real \(t\). Expand: \(0\le\|u\|^2-2t\langle u,v\rangle+t^2\|v\|^2\). This quadratic in \(t\) cannot have positive discriminant. Hence \(4\langle u,v\rangle^2-4\|u\|^2\|v\|^2\le0\), giving the inequality. Equality holds exactly when the vectors are linearly dependent.

3. Orthogonal Complement

DEFINITION + THEOREM
For a subspace \(W\le V\), \(W^\perp=\{v\in V:\langle v,w\rangle=0\text{ for all }w\in W\}\).

Orthogonal complement is a subspace

For \(x,y\in W^\perp\), scalars \(a,b\), and any \(w\in W\), \(\langle ax+by,w\rangle=a\langle x,w\rangle+b\langle y,w\rangle=0\) in a real space. Thus \(ax+by\in W^\perp\).

In finite-dimensional inner-product spaces, \(V=W\oplus W^\perp\), and \(\dim W+\dim W^\perp=\dim V\).

4. Orthogonal and Orthonormal Sets

THEOREM + PROOF
A set is orthogonal if distinct vectors have zero inner product. It is orthonormal if it is orthogonal and every vector has norm one.

Every orthogonal set of nonzero vectors is linearly independent

Suppose \(\sum_i a_iv_i=0\). Take inner product with \(v_j\): \(\sum_i a_i\langle v_i,v_j\rangle=a_j\|v_j\|^2=0\). Since \(v_j\ne0\), its squared norm is positive; therefore \(a_j=0\). This holds for each \(j\), so the set is independent.

6. Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality

THEOREM + PROOF
For all vectors \(u,v\) in an inner-product space, \(|\langle u,v\rangle|\le\|u\|\,\|v\|\).

Proof using non-negativity

If \(v=0\), the result is immediate. For \(v\ne0\), put \(z=u-\frac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\langle v,v\rangle}v\) (with the scalar placement adjusted to the chosen inner-product convention). Since \(\langle z,z\rangle\ge0\), expansion gives \(\|u\|^2-\frac{|\langle u,v\rangle|^2}{\|v\|^2}\ge0\). Multiplying by \(\|v\|^2\) and taking square roots proves the inequality. Equality holds exactly when \(u,v\) are linearly dependent.

7. Orthogonal projection and Pythagorean identity

THEOREM
If \(u\perp v\), then \(\|u+v\|^2=\|u\|^2+\|v\|^2\). In finite dimensions, each \(x\in V\) has a unique decomposition \(x=w+z\), where \(w\in W\), \(z\in W^\perp\).

Projection formula for an orthogonal basis

Let \(e_1,\ldots,e_k\) be an orthogonal basis of \(W\). Set \(w=\sum_i\frac{\langle x,e_i\rangle}{\langle e_i,e_i\rangle}e_i\) under the standard convention. Then for every \(j\), \(\langle x-w,e_j\rangle=0\), so \(x-w\in W^\perp\). Uniqueness follows because \(W\cap W^\perp=\{0\}\).

Canonical Forms

1. Algebra of Linear Transformations

DEFINITION
\(A(V)=\operatorname{Hom}(V,V)\) is the algebra of linear transformations on \(V\). Addition and scalar multiplication are pointwise; multiplication is composition: \((ST)(v)=S(T(v))\). The identity map is the multiplicative identity.

Composition is associative, but generally not commutative: \(ST\ne TS\).

2. Invertible Transformations and Eigenvalues

DEFINITIONS + THEOREM
\(T\) is invertible if there is \(T^{-1}\) with \(TT^{-1}=T^{-1}T=I\). A scalar \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue if there exists \(v\ne0\) such that \(Tv=\lambda v\); such a vector is an eigenvector.

Eigenvalue criterion

\(Tv=\lambda v\) is equivalent to \((T-\lambda I)v=0\). A nonzero solution exists exactly when \(T-\lambda I\) is singular. In a finite-dimensional space, this is equivalent to \(\det([T]-\lambda I)=0\).

3. Characteristic and Minimal Polynomials

DEFINITION
The characteristic polynomial is \(p_T(x)=\det(xI-[T])\). The minimal polynomial \(m_T(x)\) is the monic polynomial of least positive degree satisfying \(m_T(T)=0\).

The minimal polynomial divides every polynomial that annihilates \(T\), and by Cayley–Hamilton it divides the characteristic polynomial.

4. Similarity and Change of Basis

THEOREM + PROOF
Matrices \(A,B\) are similar if \(B=P^{-1}AP\) for an invertible matrix \(P\). They represent the same linear map in different bases.

Why change-of-basis matrices give similarity

Let \(P\) convert coordinates in the new basis to coordinates in the old basis. Applying \(T\) in old coordinates and converting back gives \(P^{-1}AP\). Thus the new matrix is similar to the old one.

5. Invariant Subspaces and Triangular Form

DEFINITION + THEOREM
A subspace \(W\) is invariant under \(T\) if \(T(W)\subseteq W\).
Triangularisation theorem (split characteristic polynomial). If the characteristic polynomial of a linear operator on a finite-dimensional space over \(F\) splits into linear factors over \(F\), then there is a basis in which its matrix is upper triangular.

Proof by induction on dimension

Since the characteristic polynomial splits, it has a root \(\lambda\), so there is an eigenvector \(v_1\ne0\). The one-dimensional space \(Fv_1\) is invariant. The induced operator on the quotient \(V/Fv_1\) has dimension one less and its characteristic polynomial also splits. By induction, choose a basis of the quotient giving an upper-triangular matrix. Lift that basis to vectors of \(V\), and prepend \(v_1\). In this basis the first column has only its first entry possibly nonzero, and the quotient block is upper triangular; hence the full matrix is upper triangular.

6. Nilpotent Transformations and Jordan Chains

DEFINITION + THEOREM
A transformation \(N\) is nilpotent if \(N^k=0\) for some positive integer \(k\). A Jordan chain of length \(r\) is a sequence \(v_1,\ldots,v_r\) with \(Nv_1=0\) and \(Nv_j=v_{j-1}\) for \(j\ge2\).

A nilpotent operator has only eigenvalue zero. Its Jordan blocks have zero diagonal and ones on the superdiagonal (under the stated chain convention). The block sizes are invariants and determine its similarity class.

Why a nilpotent operator has only eigenvalue zero

If \(Nv=\lambda v\) with \(v\ne0\), then \(0=N^kv=\lambda^kv\). Since \(v\ne0\), \(\lambda^k=0\), so \(\lambda=0\).

7. Trace and Determinant

DEFINITIONS + PROPERTIES
For a square matrix \(A=(a_{ij})\), \(\operatorname{tr}(A)=\sum_i a_{ii}\). The determinant is the alternating multilinear scalar associated with the matrix.

Similarity invariants: \(\operatorname{tr}(P^{-1}AP)=\operatorname{tr}(A)\) and \(\det(P^{-1}AP)=\det(A)\). Also \(\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)\) and \(\det(AB)=\det A\det B\).

8. Polynomial expressions in operators and minimal polynomial

DEFINITION + THEOREMS
For \(q(x)=a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_rx^r\), define \(q(T)=a_0I+a_1T+\cdots+a_rT^r\). The minimal polynomial \(m_T\) is the unique monic polynomial of least degree with \(m_T(T)=0\).

Divisibility property

Divide any annihilating polynomial \(q\) by \(m_T\): \(q=sm_T+r\), where \(r=0\) or \(\deg r<\deg m_T\). Substitution gives \(0=q(T)=s(T)m_T(T)+r(T)=r(T)\). Minimality forces \(r=0\); hence \(m_T\mid q\). In particular, Cayley–Hamilton implies \(m_T\mid p_T\).

THEOREM + PROOF
Eigenvectors belonging to pairwise distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent.

10. Nilpotent index, cyclic subspaces and Jordan invariants

DEFINITIONS + THEOREMS
The nilpotency index is the least positive integer \(k\) for which \(N^k=0\). A cyclic subspace generated by \(v\) is \(Z(v;N)=\operatorname{span}\{v,Nv,N^2v,\ldots\}\).

For a nilpotent operator, a Jordan chain records successive images until zero. The lengths of the Jordan chains (equivalently, the sizes of its nilpotent Jordan blocks) are invariants. Two nilpotent operators are similar exactly when their lists of block sizes agree.

Dimension drop in a cyclic chain

If a cyclic chain has length \(m\), its generating vectors are independent. Applying \(N^k\) removes the first \(k\) positions in the chain, leaving a span of dimension \(m-k\) for \(0\le k\le m\).

11. Trace, transpose and determinant identities

PROPERTIES

For square matrices, \(\operatorname{tr}(A+B)=\operatorname{tr}A+\operatorname{tr}B\), \(\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)\), and trace is invariant under similarity. Determinants satisfy \(\det(AB)=\det A\det B\), \(\det(A^T)=\det A\), and \(A\) is invertible iff \(\det A\ne0\). Transpose reverses products: \((AB)^T=B^TA^T\).

Hermitian, Unitary and Normal Transformations

1. Adjoint and Unitary Transformation

DEFINITION + THEOREM
The adjoint \(T^*\) is characterised by \(\langle Tu,v\rangle=\langle u,T^*v\rangle\) (with the inner-product convention fixed). A transformation is unitary if it preserves inner products: \(\langle Tu,Tv\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle\).

Unitary iff \(T^*T=I\)

For all \(u,v\), \(\langle Tu,Tv\rangle=\langle u,T^*Tv\rangle\). If \(T\) is unitary, this equals \(\langle u,v\rangle\), so \(\langle u,(T^*T-I)v\rangle=0\) for all \(u,v\), implying \(T^*T=I\). Conversely, if \(T^*T=I\), the displayed identity gives preservation of inner products.

2. Hermitian / Self-Adjoint and Skew-Hermitian

DEFINITIONS + THEOREM
\(T\) is Hermitian (self-adjoint) if \(T^*=T\). It is skew-Hermitian if \(T^*=-T\).

Eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator are real

Let \(Tv=\lambda v\), \(v\ne0\). Self-adjointness gives \(\langle Tv,v\rangle=\langle v,Tv\rangle\). Using conjugate symmetry, the two sides are conjugates, so \(\lambda\langle v,v\rangle=\overline\lambda\langle v,v\rangle\). Since \(\langle v,v\rangle>0\), \(\lambda=\overline\lambda\), hence \(\lambda\in\mathbb R\).

3. Normal Transformations

DEFINITION + THEOREM
An operator \(N\) is normal if \(N^*N=NN^*\).

Every unitary and every Hermitian operator is normal. In a finite-dimensional complex inner-product space, a normal operator has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors (spectral theorem).

Key step: eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal

For normal \(N\), the operator \(N-\lambda I\) is normal. A standard norm identity for normal operators gives \(\|(N-\lambda I)v\|=\|(N^* -\overline\lambda I)v\|\). If \(Nv=\lambda v\), the left side is zero, so \(N^*v=\overline\lambda v\). If \(Nw=\mu w\), then \(\langle Nv,w\rangle=\langle v,N^*w\rangle\) implies \(\lambda\langle v,w\rangle=\mu\langle v,w\rangle\) (with convention-adjusted conjugation). For distinct eigenvalues, \(\langle v,w\rangle=0\).
The spectral theorem requires the appropriate field and finite-dimensional inner-product hypotheses. Over the complex numbers, normal operators are unitarily diagonalizable; over the reals, additional care is needed for non-real eigenvalue pairs.

4. Bilinear Forms

DEFINITION + THEOREM
A bilinear form \(b:V\times V\to F\) is linear in each argument separately. It is symmetric if \(b(u,v)=b(v,u)\), alternating if \(b(v,v)=0\) for all \(v\), and non-degenerate if its radical is zero.

Relative to a basis, a bilinear form is represented by a matrix \(A\) with \(b(x,y)=x^TAy\). It is symmetric iff \(A^T=A\). Under a basis change \(P\), the representing matrix changes by congruence \(A\mapsto P^TAP\), not similarity.

Matrix criterion for non-degeneracy

In coordinates, \(b(x,y)=x^TAy\). A vector \(x\) is in the left radical exactly when \(x^TA=0\). The radical is zero iff the only solution is \(x=0\), which in finite dimension is equivalent to \(A\) being invertible, i.e. \(\det A\ne0\).

5. Unitary basis criterion

THEOREM + PROOF
A linear operator on a finite-dimensional inner-product space is unitary iff it maps an orthonormal basis to an orthonormal basis.

Proof

If \(T\) preserves inner products, then for an orthonormal basis \((e_i)\), \(\langle Te_i,Te_j\rangle=\langle e_i,e_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}\). Conversely, if \((Te_i)\) is orthonormal, write \(u=\sum a_ie_i\), \(v=\sum b_ie_i\). Expanding both inner products in these bases gives \(\langle Tu,Tv\rangle=\langle u,v\rangle\); hence \(T\) is unitary.

6. Normal operators: kernel and eigenvector facts

LEMMAS + COROLLARIES

If \(N\) is normal, then \(\|Nv\|=\|N^*v\|\). Consequently \(Nv=0\iff N^*v=0\). Applying this to \(N-\lambda I\) shows that if \(Nv=\lambda v\), then \(N^*v=\overline{\lambda}v\). Eigenvectors of a normal operator corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

7. Positive and nonnegative operators

DEFINITION + THEOREM
A Hermitian operator \(T\) is nonnegative if \(\langle Tv,v\rangle\ge0\) for every \(v\), and positive definite if the inequality is strict for every nonzero \(v\).

For a Hermitian operator in finite dimension, nonnegativity is equivalent to all eigenvalues being nonnegative; positive definiteness is equivalent to all eigenvalues being positive. Also, \(T\ge0\) iff there exists an operator \(A\) with \(T=A^*A\).

8. Bilinear form: matrix, radicals, rank and non-degeneracy

DEFINITIONS + THEOREMS

For an ordered basis, a bilinear form has a unique matrix \(A\) such that \(b(x,y)=x^TAy\). Its left and right radicals are the vectors annihilating the form in the first or second argument. The rank of the form equals the rank of its representing matrix and is independent of basis. The form is non-degenerate iff its radical is zero, equivalently its matrix is invertible.

9. Symmetric, skew-symmetric forms and diagonalisation

THEOREMS

A form is symmetric iff its matrix satisfies \(A^T=A\), and skew-symmetric iff \(A^T=-A\). Over a field of characteristic different from 2, every bilinear form decomposes into symmetric and skew-symmetric parts: \(b_s(u,v)=\frac12(b(u,v)+b(v,u))\), \(b_a(u,v)=\frac12(b(u,v)-b(v,u))\). Over the real numbers, a symmetric form can be diagonalised by a suitable change of basis; the counts of positive, negative and zero diagonal entries are invariant under congruence (Sylvester's law of inertia).

Notation and correction guide

NotationMeaning
\(F\)A field, such as \(\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\)
\(V^*\)Dual space \(\operatorname{Hom}_F(V,F)\)
\(\ker T\)Kernel / null space of a linear map
\(\operatorname{range}T\)Image of a linear map
\(W^0\)Annihilator of \(W\) in \(V^*\)
\(W^\perp\)Orthogonal complement with respect to an inner product
\(T^*\)Adjoint; not the dual-space symbol
\(P^{-1}AP\)Similarity transformation
\(P^TAP\)Congruence transformation for bilinear forms
\(\delta_{ij}\)Kronecker delta: 1 if \(i=j\), otherwise 0

Important distinction: The annihilator \(W^0\subseteq V^*\) is not the same as the orthogonal complement \(W^\perp\subseteq V\). The latter depends on an inner product.
